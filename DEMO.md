# Demo script

Eight minutes. The order matters: it moves from "it does the job" to "it does things a
person cannot" to "it learns from its manager". Hand the laptop over at step 6.

---

## 1. The job description (30s)

Open on the header. Do not open on the queue.

> "This is Aria. Travel and Expense Controller, reports to the Corporate Controller.
> She can approve up to a limit, disallow up to a smaller limit, post to the ledger,
> and refer anything above that upward. Those are her terms of employment and you can
> change them in the Terms tab. Nobody prompts her. Charges land and she works them."

## 2. Work the queue (60s)

Click **Work the queue**. Let it run. Talk over it.

> "Eighteen charges came in overnight. She is doing five things to each one: assembling
> the evidence, corroborating it against records the employee does not control, applying
> the policy, testing her own authority, then coding and posting."

Point at the touchless number as it moves.

## 3. One settled workpaper (90s)

Open a clean case, for example the Taj hotel charge.

Show, in this order:
- the narrative, in her words
- the exhibits, numbered
- **the quoted policy clause**. Say: "she cannot rely on a clause she cannot quote"
- the authority band: where the amount sat against her limit
- the balanced journal entry
- open the work log: model, tokens, latency, cost per call

> "That is a workpaper. An auditor could pick it up cold."

## 4. Edit the policy live (90s) — the important one

Go to **Policy**. Find clause 4.1. Change the metro hotel cap from 9,000 to 6,000.
Publish. Re-queue. Re-run the Taj charge.

> "Nothing about that rule is in the code. The policy is a document she reads at the
> moment she decides. Change it and she applies the new text, and quotes it back."

Offer the keyboard: **"change anything in there you like."**

## 5. What a person cannot do (60s)

Open the Pixel Print Studio charge.

> "Two identical charges at one merchant an hour apart. Clause 10.2 makes splitting a
> violation on its own. She found that by querying every charge in the company, not by
> looking at this one. A human auditor sampling two percent never sees it."

Then the Grand Meridien claim.

> "Eighteen thousand rupees, employee claim, no card record anywhere. She will not tell
> you that receipt was generated by a model, because nobody can tell you that reliably.
> She tells you nothing independent supports it."

## 6. Hand over the laptop (2 min)

> "Break it. Add a charge. Upload a receipt, make one up if you like. Change her authority
> limits. Rewrite the policy."

Sit back. Do not narrate.

## 7. The learning loop (90s) — the close

Go to **Escalations**. Pick the Pixel Print split. Write a real reason, for example:

> "Two invoices from one vendor on one day for the same amount is a split. Disallow unless
> the vendor confirms in writing they are separate engagements."

Resolve it. Then open the **Precedent book**.

> "That ruling is now a standing rule. She generalised it, not to that merchant, to the
> pattern. The next time this shape of charge appears, she decides it herself and tells
> you which ruling she followed. That is the difference between an agent that asks the
> same question forever and one that learns its manager's judgment."

## 8. How you know it works (60s)

Two places, and the order matters.

**In the product, on the queue.** Point at the panel above the charges:

> "She settled fourteen of these without me. Every one rests on a clause found word for
> word in the policy, every one closed with an entry that balances, and I can open any of
> them and say I would have decided differently."

Open one she settled and press **I would have decided the same**. The header changes.

> "That is the only honest way to trust the ones I never opened: check a few at random and
> see whether I agree. Nothing else in this screen is worth anything without it."

**Then from the slides, not the screen.** The evaluation lives under Settings because it is
something you run when the policy changes, not something you watch. Talk about it with the
numbers from your own run rather than clicking through it:

> "Before any of this goes near a customer we run six suites. The one that matters is
> policy invariance: the same charge put to her under five different rulebooks, and the
> answer has to follow the book she was given. An agent carrying a memorised industry
> default passes a single-policy test and fails that one.
>
> And on every run so far, the count of charges allowed in full that should not have been
> paid is zero."

---

## If something breaks

Say what happened, plainly, and keep going. A visible failure handled calmly is a better
signal than a demo that never wobbles. The escalation queue is itself the answer: when she
cannot do something, a human gets it with the evidence attached.

## Questions to expect, and the honest answers

**"Is any of this hardcoded?"**
Judgment, no. There is no rules engine. Authority limits and double-entry arithmetic are
in code on purpose, because those should not be a model's opinion.

**"Is the card feed real?"**
No. There is no live card network in a take-home. The corpus is local. Every decision made
about it is real, and you can add to the corpus yourself.

**"Can it detect AI-generated receipts?"**
No, and neither can anything else, reliably. It corroborates against records the claimant
does not control. That is a deliberate product position, not a gap.

**"Why not just build a better Concur?"**
Because the pre-spend half is commoditised. Every incumbent ships OCR, categorisation,
conversational booking and autonomous approval. The post-spend half is still people, and it
needs reasoning across card, travel, HRIS, ERP and local tax together, which no single-silo
vendor can do.
